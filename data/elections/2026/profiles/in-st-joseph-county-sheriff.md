# Indiana County Sheriff — 2026 race profile

_Researched 2026-10-11 · status: draft._ Pending editorial review.

## About the office

The sheriff is an elected county officer created by the Indiana Constitution who arrests offenders, keeps the peace, serves court papers, keeps order in county courts and runs the county jail [1, 2]. The Indiana Sheriffs' Association describes sheriffs as constitutional officers elected by the people, whose work includes operating jails, serving civil process, providing court security and coordinating emergency response [3]. Every county elects its sheriff in 2026 for a four-year term, and no one may serve as sheriff more than eight years in any twelve-year period [1].

- **Term:** 4 years; the sheriff serves until a successor is elected and qualified [4, 1]
- **Elected:** Countywide by the county's voters on the partisan general election ballot; all county sheriffs are elected in 2026 [1]
- **Term limit:** No more than eight years in any period of twelve years (Indiana Constitution, Article 6, Section 2) [1]
- **Qualifications:** Must have lived in the county for at least one year before the election, be registered to vote in the county by the filing deadline, and file a statement of economic interests; a sheriff who moves out of the county forfeits the office [1, 4]
- **Pay:** A county may pay the sheriff a salary from the county general fund of at least 50% to 100% of the state minimum salary of a full-time prosecuting attorney, depending on county population [5]
- **On the 2026 ballot:** 92 races; 26 contested [6]

### What the office does

- Arrests without a warrant people who commit an offense in the sheriff's view, brings them before a county court, and holds them while the arrest is investigated [2].
- Suppresses breaches of the peace and pursues and jails felons [2].
- Takes care of the county jail and the prisoners held there [2, 3].
- Serves and carries out legal papers (process) directed to the sheriff by courts and the county commissioners [2].
- Attends and keeps order in all courts of the county, which the Sheriffs' Association describes as providing court security [2, 3].
- Takes photographs, fingerprints and other identification data from people held for crimes, and DNA samples for felonies [2].
- Reports twice a year to the Indiana Department of Correction the average daily cost of holding a jail prisoner [2].
- Holds state prisoners, including people convicted of Level 6 felonies, in the county jail, for which the state reimburses counties at a daily rate set in the state budget [7].
- Coordinates emergency response and works with local police, prosecutors and community partners [3].
- In a county whose council creates a sheriff's merit board by ordinance, appoints three of its five members, while the county police officers elect the other two; no more than two sheriff appointees and one elected member may belong to the same party [8].
- Must report information to federal authorities when a person arrested for a felony or misdemeanor is believed to lack legal immigration status, and, since July 2026, notify the judge setting bail when holding a person subject to an immigration detainer [9, 10].
- May supervise and inspect businesses such as pawnbrokers and secondhand dealers [2].
- What the sheriff does not control: the county commissioners must establish and maintain the county jail building [11]; the county makes the appropriation for feeding prisoners and sets the sheriff's salary [5]; the prosecuting attorney decides which crimes to prosecute [12]; and judges decide bail [13].

### Recent changes to the office

- February 2025: The Indiana Department of Correction said funding to reimburse county jails for holding Level 6 felons and state prisoners, then $40 per day, was exhausted for state fiscal year 2025; Tippecanoe County Sheriff Robert Goldsmith, then president of the Indiana Sheriffs' Association, called it very disappointing, and Rep. Greg Steuerwald (R-Avon) said everything owed would be paid [14].
- 2025: The new state budget raised the daily rate for Level 6 offenders to $42, raised the jail reimbursement line to $45 million and then $41 million a year, and moved to direct reimbursement with costs submitted within 60 days; Sheriffs' Association executive director Stephen Luce called the rate better than nothing, citing an association study that put the average cost at about $74 per inmate per day [7].
- July 2025: The Department of Correction planned to begin paying about $12.7 million owed to county jails for housing state offenders [7].
- July 2025: House Enrolled Act 1393 took effect, requiring jails to alert federal immigration authorities, with the county sheriff passing the information to federal officials, after felony or misdemeanor arrests when there is probable cause the person lacks legal status [9].
- July 2026: Senate Bill 76 took effect, requiring sheriff's offices and police departments to notify the bail judge when they hold a person subject to an immigration detainer and barring local policies that restrict cooperation with federal officials; St. Joseph County Sheriff Bill Redman said his county already cooperates with ICE, while South Bend Mayor James Mueller said the city will protect the constitutional rights of everyone living there [10].
- January 2026: The Sheriffs' Association's 2026 president, Porter County Sheriff Jeffrey Balon, listed jail overcrowding, staffing shortages, the drug epidemic and mental illness among the issues sheriffs raise with legislators [3].
- November 2026: Public Question 1 asks voters whether courts may deny bail, for offenses other than murder or treason, when the state proves by clear and convincing evidence that no release conditions will reasonably protect others or the community [15]; Sen. Eric Koch (R-Bedford) said releasing some defendants before trial is too dangerous, while Zach Stock of the Indiana Public Defender Council raised concerns about jail overcrowding [13].

## Current officeholder: Bill Redman (Democratic)

Bill Redman, a Democrat, was elected St. Joseph County sheriff in November 2018 and re-elected in November 2022 [16, 17]. He is barred by term limits from running again in 2026 and is running for county commissioner instead [18, 19].

- November 2018: Won the sheriff's race as the Democratic nominee with about 59% of the vote over Paul Jonas, with 97% of precincts reporting [16].
- November 2022: Re-elected over Republican Kevin McGowan; ABC57's candidate summary listed his platform as addressing violence through community involvement and upgraded equipment, drug prevention, treatment and enforcement, reducing the jail population through a mental health clinic and a Jail Chemical Addiction Program, and working with local, state and federal partners [17].
- February 2024: Redman told WVPE the jail was down about 50 jailers, compared with 4 of 145 correction officer positions unfilled before the 2020 pandemic. The county council had already raised correction officer base pay 12% that year, from $41,000 to $45,000; Redman proposed a further 20% raise to $54,000, saying higher pay would attract applicants and keep veteran jailers. The department had needed $700,000 from the council to cover overtime at the end of 2023 [20].
- 2026: Redman approved a jail hiring program, described by Lt. Earl Wigfall, to hire 18-year-old graduates from the South Bend Community School Corporation as correctional staff [19].

## This race

- This is an open seat: Sheriff Bill Redman cannot seek re-election because of term limits [18, 19]. The St. Joseph County Police Department is the sheriff's department; it also runs the county jail [19, 20].
- In the May 5, 2026 Democratic primary, South Bend Police Chief Scott Ruszkowski won about 66% of the vote over county police Lt. Earl Wigfall (about 34%), according to unofficial results [21]. Republican Rod Laureys, a county police corporal, ran unopposed for his party's nomination [21, 19].
- Jail staffing was a shared theme in the primary: Wigfall called it his top priority, Ruszkowski called it a priority, and Laureys proposed reopening the jail to clergy and ministry visits [19]. Laureys has emphasized a new drug unit and officer morale; Ruszkowski has emphasized gun violence, regional cooperation and community outreach [19, 21].
- No general-election forum or debate between Ruszkowski and Laureys was found in available coverage.

## Rod Laureys

Rod Laureys is a St. Joseph County police corporal and U.S. Marine Corps veteran who worked 10 years as a federal ATF task force officer; he is the Republican nominee [19, 22].

### Background

- Corporal with the St. Joseph County Police Department, with 15 years at the department, including 10 years as a federal ATF (Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives) task force officer [19, 22].
- U.S. Marine Corps veteran and former South Bend police officer [19, 23].

### Path to the ballot

- Filed for sheriff on January 8, 2026 [23].
- Ran unopposed in the May 5, 2026 Republican primary [21]; listed on the general-election ballot as a Republican [6].

### Positions & plans

**Jail & corrections**

- Wants to reopen the jail to clergy and ministry visits, which he says were halted during COVID-19 [19].

**Drugs**

- Has said 'There's no drug unit established in St. Joseph County' and proposes creating one [19].
- At a July 2026 county Republican briefing, named drug trafficking as a top agenda item and said agencies working toward the same goals get more done; ABC57 reported he said minor recreational marijuana users would not be dealt with harshly but appropriate charges would still be filed [22].

**Patrol & response**

- Wants stronger overall training and better distribution of field officers across the county [19].
- Said he would lead from the front, personally joining officers on the street [23].

**Staffing & pay**

- Says restoring integrity, trust and morale in the department is a priority [19, 23].

### On the current officeholder

- Has said the department's morale has declined under the current sheriff [23], and told ABC57, 'The troops working in the field don't have trust in this administration' [19]. Laureys also raised a legal matter involving the sheriff; details are held for editorial review.

### How they say they'd run the office

- Create a county drug unit [19].
- Strengthen training and redistribute field officers across the county [19].
- Work closely with the prosecutor's office; at the July 2026 briefing he said there has been 'a huge breakdown between the prosecutor's office and the police department, particularly the city' and that he would not tolerate it [22].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- Appeared at a July 31, 2026 St. Joseph County Republican Party briefing with Republican prosecutor candidate John Maciejczyk, whom he praised [22].
- No endorsements from law-enforcement groups or campaign-finance totals were found in available coverage.

### What we couldn't verify

- No campaign website or social-media page was located.
- No stated positions found on the budget, school safety, immigration enforcement or jail pay.
- Campaign-finance reports were not reviewed.

## Scott Ruszkowski

Scott Ruszkowski is South Bend's police chief, with about 37 years at the South Bend Police Department and 11 as chief, and a Navy veteran; he is the Democratic nominee [19, 21].

### Background

- Chief of the South Bend Police Department for 11 years, with about 37 years at the department [19, 24]; WNDU described it as nearly 38 years [21].
- U.S. Navy veteran [21].
- Says he has 15 years of budget and administrative experience and 30 years of managing people [25].
- Is retiring as South Bend police chief at the end of 2026 regardless of the election outcome [24].

### Path to the ballot

- Announced his campaign on January 23, 2026, after an exploratory committee [26].
- Won the May 5, 2026 Democratic primary with about 66% of the vote over Lt. Earl Wigfall, according to unofficial results [21]; listed on the general-election ballot as a Democrat [6].

### Positions & plans

**Jail & corrections**

- Says jail staffing is a priority, noting that correctional officer recruitment and retention is a nationwide struggle, and stresses rewarding and supporting jail staff [19].

**Patrol & response**

- Calls gun violence the top crime issue — 'When it comes to crime, gun violence is number one, no doubt' — and calls for community intervention before violence occurs [25].
- Calls for much closer collaboration among county, South Bend and Mishawaka agencies and greater use of community resources [21].
- Supports continuing de-escalation and crisis intervention training [25].

**Staffing & pay**

- Emphasizes mentoring and supporting officers [19].

**Transparency**

- Wants more community outreach at the county police department, possibly including an online transparency tool modeled on South Bend's [19].

### On the current officeholder

- Has said he talks regularly with Sheriff Redman; no criticism of Redman's record was found [26].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Gather input from county police personnel and the community before making changes [26].
- Increase regional cooperation on gun violence across the county [21].
- Consider an online transparency tool for the county police department [19].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- Credited many of the area's Democratic elected officials with encouraging him to run [26].
- No formal endorsements from law-enforcement groups or campaign-finance totals were found in available coverage.

### What we couldn't verify

- No campaign website was located.
- No stated positions found on the budget, drugs, school safety or immigration enforcement.
- Campaign-finance reports were not reviewed.
- Some outlets spell his name 'Ruzskowski'; the official ballot spelling is Ruszkowski.
