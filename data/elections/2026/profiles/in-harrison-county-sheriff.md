# Indiana County Sheriff — 2026 race profile

_Researched 2026-10-11 · status: draft._ Pending editorial review.

## About the office

The sheriff is an elected county officer created by the Indiana Constitution who arrests offenders, keeps the peace, serves court papers, keeps order in county courts and runs the county jail [1, 2]. The Indiana Sheriffs' Association describes sheriffs as constitutional officers elected by the people, whose work includes operating jails, serving civil process, providing court security and coordinating emergency response [3]. Every county elects its sheriff in 2026 for a four-year term, and no one may serve as sheriff more than eight years in any twelve-year period [1].

- **Term:** 4 years; the sheriff serves until a successor is elected and qualified [4, 1]
- **Elected:** Countywide by the county's voters on the partisan general election ballot; all county sheriffs are elected in 2026 [1]
- **Term limit:** No more than eight years in any period of twelve years (Indiana Constitution, Article 6, Section 2) [1]
- **Qualifications:** Must have lived in the county for at least one year before the election, be registered to vote in the county by the filing deadline, and file a statement of economic interests; a sheriff who moves out of the county forfeits the office [1, 4]
- **Pay:** A county may pay the sheriff a salary from the county general fund of at least 50% to 100% of the state minimum salary of a full-time prosecuting attorney, depending on county population [5]
- **On the 2026 ballot:** 92 races; 26 contested [6]

### What the office does

- Arrests without a warrant people who commit an offense in the sheriff's view, brings them before a county court, and holds them while the arrest is investigated [2].
- Suppresses breaches of the peace and pursues and jails felons [2].
- Takes care of the county jail and the prisoners held there [2, 3].
- Serves and carries out legal papers (process) directed to the sheriff by courts and the county commissioners [2].
- Attends and keeps order in all courts of the county, which the Sheriffs' Association describes as providing court security [2, 3].
- Takes photographs, fingerprints and other identification data from people held for crimes, and DNA samples for felonies [2].
- Reports twice a year to the Indiana Department of Correction the average daily cost of holding a jail prisoner [2].
- Holds state prisoners, including people convicted of Level 6 felonies, in the county jail, for which the state reimburses counties at a daily rate set in the state budget [7].
- Coordinates emergency response and works with local police, prosecutors and community partners [3].
- In a county whose council creates a sheriff's merit board by ordinance, appoints three of its five members, while the county police officers elect the other two; no more than two sheriff appointees and one elected member may belong to the same party [8].
- Must report information to federal authorities when a person arrested for a felony or misdemeanor is believed to lack legal immigration status, and, since July 2026, notify the judge setting bail when holding a person subject to an immigration detainer [9, 10].
- May supervise and inspect businesses such as pawnbrokers and secondhand dealers [2].
- What the sheriff does not control: the county commissioners must establish and maintain the county jail building [11]; the county makes the appropriation for feeding prisoners and sets the sheriff's salary [5]; the prosecuting attorney decides which crimes to prosecute [12]; and judges decide bail [13].

### Recent changes to the office

- February 2025: The Indiana Department of Correction said funding to reimburse county jails for holding Level 6 felons and state prisoners, then $40 per day, was exhausted for state fiscal year 2025; Tippecanoe County Sheriff Robert Goldsmith, then president of the Indiana Sheriffs' Association, called it very disappointing, and Rep. Greg Steuerwald (R-Avon) said everything owed would be paid [14].
- 2025: The new state budget raised the daily rate for Level 6 offenders to $42, raised the jail reimbursement line to $45 million and then $41 million a year, and moved to direct reimbursement with costs submitted within 60 days; Sheriffs' Association executive director Stephen Luce called the rate better than nothing, citing an association study that put the average cost at about $74 per inmate per day [7].
- July 2025: The Department of Correction planned to begin paying about $12.7 million owed to county jails for housing state offenders [7].
- July 2025: House Enrolled Act 1393 took effect, requiring jails to alert federal immigration authorities, with the county sheriff passing the information to federal officials, after felony or misdemeanor arrests when there is probable cause the person lacks legal status [9].
- July 2026: Senate Bill 76 took effect, requiring sheriff's offices and police departments to notify the bail judge when they hold a person subject to an immigration detainer and barring local policies that restrict cooperation with federal officials; St. Joseph County Sheriff Bill Redman said his county already cooperates with ICE, while South Bend Mayor James Mueller said the city will protect the constitutional rights of everyone living there [10].
- January 2026: The Sheriffs' Association's 2026 president, Porter County Sheriff Jeffrey Balon, listed jail overcrowding, staffing shortages, the drug epidemic and mental illness among the issues sheriffs raise with legislators [3].
- November 2026: Public Question 1 asks voters whether courts may deny bail, for offenses other than murder or treason, when the state proves by clear and convincing evidence that no release conditions will reasonably protect others or the community [15]; Sen. Eric Koch (R-Bedford) said releasing some defendants before trial is too dangerous, while Zach Stock of the Indiana Public Defender Council raised concerns about jail overcrowding [13].

## Current officeholder: Nick Smith (Republican)

Nick Smith is the current Harrison County sheriff. He was elected in 2018 over Democrat Brad Shepherd and re-elected without a Democratic opponent in 2022 [16, 17]. He is not on the 2026 ballot. Republican nominee Brad R. Rothrock serves as his chief deputy [18, 6].

- November 2018: Elected sheriff with 9,719 votes (62.37%) to 5,863 (37.63%) for Democrat Brad Shepherd, according to the county's cumulative results report [16].
- May 2022: Unopposed in the Republican primary with 3,771 votes in the county's unofficial cumulative results [19].
- November 2022: Re-elected with 11,093 votes. The county's results report notes that no Democrat filed for sheriff [17].
- 2026: Not a candidate. He has served two consecutive terms, and Indiana limits sheriffs to two consecutive terms [16, 17, 6].

## This race

- Harrison County (county seat: Corydon) will elect a sheriff on Nov. 3, 2026. This is an open seat: Sheriff Nick Smith, elected in 2018 and re-elected in 2022, is not on the ballot [16, 17, 6].
- Brad R. Rothrock was the only sheriff candidate on the county's primary candidate list. He filed as a Republican on Jan. 7, 2026, and no Democrat appears on that list for sheriff [20]. Rothrock was unopposed in the May 5 Republican primary and received 2,997 votes in the county's unofficial cumulative results report, run May 15, 2026 [21].
- Democrat Jim Slucher is on the general-election list but was not on the primary candidate list [6, 20]. Sources reviewed do not say how he was placed on the ballot.
- The Corydon Democrat sent both candidates the same questionnaire in August 2026 [22]. Rothrock's top priorities are training, equipment and staff retention, along with dismantling local drug networks. He would build on what he calls the department's 'strong foundation' [18]. Slucher's top priorities are drug enforcement, a 'strong presence' in schools, youth drug-awareness programs and acting on citizens' input [23].
- Main differences in background: Rothrock has worked at the sheriff's department since 2012 and is now its chief deputy [18]. Slucher is self-employed in auto repair. He cites his life, management and public-facing work experience, and lists no law-enforcement experience in the questionnaire [23].
- No candidate forums or debates were found in sources reviewed.

## Brad R. Rothrock

Republican Brad R. Rothrock is chief deputy of the Harrison County Sheriff's Department and has worked there since 2012 [18]. He was unopposed in the May 2026 primary [21].

### Background

- Employment: chief deputy at the Harrison County Sheriff's Department [18].
- He has worked at the department since July 2012. He has served as a deputy on all three shifts, as a narcotics detective and with a narcotics K-9, and as chief deputy under Sheriff Nick Smith. He also spent nine years on the regional SWAT team [18].
- Education: associate degree in criminal justice. Political experience: none listed. Age 35 at the time of the questionnaire [18].

### Path to the ballot

- Filed as a Republican for Harrison County Sheriff on Jan. 7, 2026 [20].
- Unopposed in the May 5, 2026 Republican primary, with 2,997 votes in the county's unofficial cumulative results [21].

### Positions & plans

**Staffing & pay**

- Says his top priority is giving residents 'personnel who are well-trained, professional and forward-thinking.' He plans to improve training and equipment and to 'recruit, train and retain the best officers' [18].

**Drugs**

- Wants to continue 'dismantling local drug networks.' He describes the local drug problem as 'a key driver in other criminal activity' [18].

**Transparency**

- Says he will work to build a relationship between the department and the community 'that stands on trust and transparency' [18].

### On the current officeholder

- Rothrock is chief deputy under Sheriff Nick Smith. He says the department 'has a strong foundation that I intend to continue building from' and describes its deputies as 'a strong group' [18].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Improve training and equipment, and give deputies the resources they need [18].
- Keep focusing on local drug networks, and work with the community so the county remains 'a safe place to live, work and play' [18].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- No endorsements or campaign-finance reports were found in sources reviewed.

### What we couldn't verify

- No campaign website was found. A third-party listing points to a campaign Facebook page (rothrock4sheriff), which was not reviewed.
- No positions found on the jail, budget requests to the county council, school resource officers or immigration-detainer agreements.
- No endorsements, fundraising reports or forum appearances found.
- Coverage is limited to one newspaper questionnaire and county election records.

## Jim Slucher

Democrat Jim Slucher is self-employed in auto repair. He says this is his third run for Harrison County sheriff and lists drug enforcement and school safety as his priorities [23].

### Background

- Employment: self-employed, auto repair facility [23].
- Education: North Harrison High School, Prosser School of Technology, plus other certificates and licensing courses he did not specify [23].
- For qualifications, he cites 60 years of life experience, learning trades from a young age, managing people, workloads and inventory, and working with the public. He lists no law-enforcement experience in the questionnaire [23].
- Political experience: says this is his 'third time running for Harrison County Sheriff' and describes himself as a member of the Democratic Party and 'a strong union supporter' [23].

### Path to the ballot

- On the general-election ballot as the Democratic candidate [6]. He was not on the county's primary candidate list, which had no Democratic sheriff candidate [20].

### Positions & plans

**Drugs**

- Lists drug enforcement as his No. 1 priority. He names fentanyl, methamphetamine, heroin and cocaine, as well as illegal prescription-drug use and sales, and links drug activity to theft [23].
- Would 'institute as many drug awareness programs as possible' for young people [23].

**School safety**

- Says he will 'make a strong presence known in our school system to ensure the safety of our children at all times' [23].

**Transparency**

- Says he would 'implement actions that will actually come from the ideas and concerns of the citizens' and run the office with 'the citizens' input' [23].

### On the current officeholder

- Slucher says he would bring 'a new perspective' to the office. He says he will 'take this office back to protect and serve with good police work and have the citizens' input' [23].
- On drug enforcement, he says the department will 'diligently continue with these pursuits as the previous sheriffs have' [23].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Focus on drug trafficking and illegal prescription-drug sales [23].
- Increase the department's presence in schools and expand youth drug-awareness programs [23].
- Base office actions on citizens' concerns [23].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- No endorsements or campaign-finance reports were found in sources reviewed.

### What we couldn't verify

- Sources reviewed do not say how he was placed on the general-election ballot (for example, by a Democratic Party vacancy filling after the primary).
- He says this is his third run for sheriff. He was not the Democratic nominee in 2018 (Brad Shepherd was) or in 2022 (no Democrat filed), so his earlier runs could not be identified or dated.
- No campaign website or social-media page was found.
- No positions found on the jail, staffing and pay, the budget or immigration-detainer agreements.
- No endorsements, fundraising reports or forum appearances found.
- Coverage is limited to one newspaper questionnaire.
