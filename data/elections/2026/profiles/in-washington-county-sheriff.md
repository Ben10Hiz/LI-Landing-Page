# Indiana County Sheriff — 2026 race profile

_Researched 2026-10-11 · status: draft._ Pending editorial review.

## About the office

The sheriff is an elected county officer created by the Indiana Constitution who arrests offenders, keeps the peace, serves court papers, keeps order in county courts and runs the county jail [1, 2]. The Indiana Sheriffs' Association describes sheriffs as constitutional officers elected by the people, whose work includes operating jails, serving civil process, providing court security and coordinating emergency response [3]. Every county elects its sheriff in 2026 for a four-year term, and no one may serve as sheriff more than eight years in any twelve-year period [1].

- **Term:** 4 years; the sheriff serves until a successor is elected and qualified [4, 1]
- **Elected:** Countywide by the county's voters on the partisan general election ballot; all county sheriffs are elected in 2026 [1]
- **Term limit:** No more than eight years in any period of twelve years (Indiana Constitution, Article 6, Section 2) [1]
- **Qualifications:** Must have lived in the county for at least one year before the election, be registered to vote in the county by the filing deadline, and file a statement of economic interests; a sheriff who moves out of the county forfeits the office [1, 4]
- **Pay:** A county may pay the sheriff a salary from the county general fund of at least 50% to 100% of the state minimum salary of a full-time prosecuting attorney, depending on county population [5]
- **On the 2026 ballot:** 92 races; 26 contested [6]

### What the office does

- Arrests without a warrant people who commit an offense in the sheriff's view, brings them before a county court, and holds them while the arrest is investigated [2].
- Suppresses breaches of the peace and pursues and jails felons [2].
- Takes care of the county jail and the prisoners held there [2, 3].
- Serves and carries out legal papers (process) directed to the sheriff by courts and the county commissioners [2].
- Attends and keeps order in all courts of the county, which the Sheriffs' Association describes as providing court security [2, 3].
- Takes photographs, fingerprints and other identification data from people held for crimes, and DNA samples for felonies [2].
- Reports twice a year to the Indiana Department of Correction the average daily cost of holding a jail prisoner [2].
- Holds state prisoners, including people convicted of Level 6 felonies, in the county jail, for which the state reimburses counties at a daily rate set in the state budget [7].
- Coordinates emergency response and works with local police, prosecutors and community partners [3].
- In a county whose council creates a sheriff's merit board by ordinance, appoints three of its five members, while the county police officers elect the other two; no more than two sheriff appointees and one elected member may belong to the same party [8].
- Must report information to federal authorities when a person arrested for a felony or misdemeanor is believed to lack legal immigration status, and, since July 2026, notify the judge setting bail when holding a person subject to an immigration detainer [9, 10].
- May supervise and inspect businesses such as pawnbrokers and secondhand dealers [2].
- What the sheriff does not control: the county commissioners must establish and maintain the county jail building [11]; the county makes the appropriation for feeding prisoners and sets the sheriff's salary [5]; the prosecuting attorney decides which crimes to prosecute [12]; and judges decide bail [13].

### Recent changes to the office

- February 2025: The Indiana Department of Correction said funding to reimburse county jails for holding Level 6 felons and state prisoners, then $40 per day, was exhausted for state fiscal year 2025; Tippecanoe County Sheriff Robert Goldsmith, then president of the Indiana Sheriffs' Association, called it very disappointing, and Rep. Greg Steuerwald (R-Avon) said everything owed would be paid [14].
- 2025: The new state budget raised the daily rate for Level 6 offenders to $42, raised the jail reimbursement line to $45 million and then $41 million a year, and moved to direct reimbursement with costs submitted within 60 days; Sheriffs' Association executive director Stephen Luce called the rate better than nothing, citing an association study that put the average cost at about $74 per inmate per day [7].
- July 2025: The Department of Correction planned to begin paying about $12.7 million owed to county jails for housing state offenders [7].
- July 2025: House Enrolled Act 1393 took effect, requiring jails to alert federal immigration authorities, with the county sheriff passing the information to federal officials, after felony or misdemeanor arrests when there is probable cause the person lacks legal status [9].
- July 2026: Senate Bill 76 took effect, requiring sheriff's offices and police departments to notify the bail judge when they hold a person subject to an immigration detainer and barring local policies that restrict cooperation with federal officials; St. Joseph County Sheriff Bill Redman said his county already cooperates with ICE, while South Bend Mayor James Mueller said the city will protect the constitutional rights of everyone living there [10].
- January 2026: The Sheriffs' Association's 2026 president, Porter County Sheriff Jeffrey Balon, listed jail overcrowding, staffing shortages, the drug epidemic and mental illness among the issues sheriffs raise with legislators [3].
- November 2026: Public Question 1 asks voters whether courts may deny bail, for offenses other than murder or treason, when the state proves by clear and convincing evidence that no release conditions will reasonably protect others or the community [15]; Sen. Eric Koch (R-Bedford) said releasing some defendants before trial is too dangerous, while Zach Stock of the Indiana Public Defender Council raised concerns about jail overcrowding [13].

## Current officeholder: Brent Miller (Republican)

Brent Miller has been Washington County sheriff since winning the 2018 election and sought re-election as a Republican in 2022; he is not on the 2026 ballot [16, 17, 18].

- 2018: Won election as sheriff after more than 30 years with the sheriff's department [16].
- January 2022: Filed for the Republican nomination for a second term; Deputy Wayne Blevins filed against him in the May 2022 primary [16].
- 2026: The sheriff's office website lists Miller as sheriff overseeing a department of more than 55 employees [17].
- 2026: The county council approved a salary-ordinance amendment moving a probationary sheriff's deputy to regular deputy pay while setting the 2027 budget [19].

## This race

- Washington County (Salem) will elect a new sheriff: Sheriff Brent Miller is not among the four candidates on the general-election ballot [18, 6].
- Four Republicans filed by January 2026 (Chris Strange, Wayne Blevins, Brad Naugle and Danny Libka), and Joe Keltner also appeared on the May 5, 2026 Republican primary ballot; Max Greene was the only Democrat [20, 21].
- Blevins won the Republican nomination; Greene was unopposed for the Democratic nomination. Claude C. Combs and Walter (Johnny Z) Zelivetz are on the ballot as independents [18, 22, 6].
- Common themes in candidate interviews with The Washington County Times include staff retention and pay, jail commissary operations, and the county's use of Flock license-plate-reader cameras, which Blevins, Greene and Zelivetz each questioned [22, 23, 24].
- No general-election forum or debate was found in available coverage.

## Max Greene

Max Greene, a military veteran and former manufacturing supervisor, is the Democratic nominee and has focused on surveillance cameras, jail finances and oversight of county government [23].

### Background

- Military veteran; former supervisor at Netshape, overseeing a machine room that ran three shifts a day, seven days a week [23].
- No law-enforcement experience was described in his candidate interview [23].

### Path to the ballot

- Only Democratic candidate on the May 5, 2026 primary ballot [21]; listed as the Democratic nominee on the general ballot [18, 6].

### Positions & plans

**Transparency**

- Would remove 'All the County Flock Cameras, or Any Surveillance Cameras for that Matter' on his first day, citing the Fourth Amendment and saying the cameras have produced wrong information and false stops [23].
- Proposes clear human-resources policies and a citizen merit board instead of one made up of law-enforcement members [23].

**Jail & corrections**

- Would rent unused jail beds to other counties and use the revenue for staff pay and retention incentives [23].
- Says the commissary should be run for safety rather than profit, saying it 'is being used as a for Profit situation on a captive population' [23].

**Staffing & pay**

- Says clear, fairly applied policies would help attract and keep staff, and favors pay tied to workload rather than general raises [23].

**Drugs**

- As sheriff he would sit on the county's JRAC board overseeing about $400,000 in opioid-settlement funds, and would direct them toward treatment after studying programs in other counties [23].

### On the current officeholder

- Criticized the jail commissary as being run for profit and called for new HR policies in the department; his other statements about the current sheriff are held for editorial review [23].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Remove county surveillance cameras on day one [23].
- Rent unused jail beds to other counties to fund staff retention [23].
- Create a citizen merit board [23].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- No endorsements or campaign-finance information was found.

### What we couldn't verify

- The interview's date shows only 'Sep 21' without a year; context indicates 2026.
- Much of his interview addresses county government beyond the sheriff's office (zoning board, auditor's office); those claims were not verified.
- No campaign website or forum appearances found; positions on patrol, school safety and immigration enforcement not found.

## Walter (Johnny Z) Zelivetz

Walter (Johnny Z) Zelivetz is running as an independent and has emphasized drug enforcement and education, officer retention and limits on license-plate cameras [24].

### Background

- His candidate interview did not describe his occupation or any law-enforcement experience; he describes himself as 'not a politician' but 'a people person' [24].

### Path to the ballot

- Says he first sought the Republican nomination and is now running as an independent [24]; listed on the general ballot as an independent [18, 6].

### Positions & plans

**Drugs**

- Supports more fentanyl education and stepped-up drug enforcement [24].

**Jail & corrections**

- Would observe jail operations, bring in a consultant to review files and interview every officer before making changes; supports treatment programs to help inmates re-enter the community [24].

**Staffing & pay**

- Calls for faster removal of poorly performing officers and better pay to retain good ones [24].

**Transparency**

- Says broad use of Flock cameras would 'violate the Fourth Amendment'; would allow them at sensitive sites such as schools, parks, hospitals and nursing homes but not to monitor ordinary residents [24].

### On the current officeholder

- Did not name the current sheriff in his interview; said he would serve the whole county, 'not just the Salem circle' [24].

### How they say they'd run the office

- First priorities: listen to taxpayers, protect good officers, help inmates re-enter society and serve the entire county [24].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- No endorsements or campaign-finance information was found.

### What we couldn't verify

- No background, occupation or law-enforcement experience found.
- He was not on the May 2026 Republican primary ballot list; when and how he left the Republican race and qualified as an independent was not found.
- The interview's date shows only 'Sep 23' without a year; context indicates 2026.
- No positions found on budget, patrol, school safety or immigration enforcement.

## Wayne Blevins

Wayne Blevins is a lieutenant with the Washington County Sheriff's Department, where he has served about 31 years, mostly on patrol; this is his third run for sheriff [22].

### Background

- Reserve deputy 1995–2000, process server from 2000, and merit deputy from 2001 after graduating from the Indiana Law Enforcement Academy [25].
- Currently holds the rank of lieutenant; describes his career as a road (patrol) officer without an administrative role [22, 25].
- Previously worked as an EMT/advanced EMT on the county ambulance service, as a deputy coroner for four years and as a volunteer firefighter [25, 22].

### Path to the ballot

- Ran for sheriff in 2018 and 2022 [22]; in 2022 he challenged Sheriff Miller in the Republican primary [16].
- Won the May 5, 2026 Republican primary in a five-candidate field [21, 18, 22].

### Positions & plans

**Staffing & pay**

- Calls employee retention a top concern; would work with the county council on competitive pay, better training and fair treatment [22].
- Says 'Drawing candidates to small departments with less pay and benefits than bigger departments can be very difficult,' and proposes recruiting people who want long-term careers there [22].

**Jail & corrections**

- Says day-to-day operations and the jail are working well; would ensure jail staff receive training [22].
- Would review all vendor and commissary contracts to control costs and keep commissary prices affordable [22].

**Drugs**

- Would target suppliers, add patrols in areas of drug activity and act on tips; would expand substance-abuse programs in the jail with referrals on release [22].
- Said in 2025 he would use officer discretion for minor misdemeanors without victims [25].

**School safety**

- Supports prevention programs in schools with resource officers and wants to bring back school visits he says were discontinued [22, 25].

**Transparency**

- Says 'I personally am not a fan of what they are becoming' about license-plate-reader cameras, citing Fourth Amendment and data-sharing concerns while acknowledging their investigative use [22].
- Wants equal treatment of employees, accountability and protection for whistleblowers [25].

### On the current officeholder

- Has said he has seen employees leave the department under the four sheriffs he served, adding, 'I'm not saying that any of the other sheriffs have done anything wrong, though' [25].
- In 2026 he said the jail and day-to-day operations are working well and he would focus on communication with employees [22].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Meet with employees and improve communication [22].
- Ongoing training in de-escalation, mental-health crisis response and constitutional duties [22].
- Study jail practices in counties such as Scott and Jackson [25].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- No endorsements or campaign-finance information was found.

### What we couldn't verify

- Primary vote totals were not found; his nomination is confirmed by the general-ballot list.
- A search snippet said he lost the 2018 Republican primary by 11 votes; unverified.
- His 2026 interview's wording on license-plate readers ('begin the process to remove them') is ambiguous about whether it refers to the cameras.
- No positions found on immigration enforcement.
