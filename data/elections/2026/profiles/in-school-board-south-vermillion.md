# Indiana School Board Member — 2026 race profile

_Researched 2026-10-10 · status: draft._ Pending editorial review.

## About the office

A school board is the elected or appointed governing body of a public school corporation, the legal unit that runs a local school district [1, 2]. The board hires and evaluates the superintendent, adopts policies, and approves the annual budget, while the superintendent and staff run schools day to day [3]. Most boards have five or seven members serving staggered four-year terms [3]. Starting in 2026, candidates may list a political party, run as independents, or list no affiliation on the ballot, and there is no primary for these seats [2, 4].

- **Term:** 4 years, with elections staggered so part of the board is chosen every two years [3]
- **Elected:** At the November general election (November 3, 2026, or November 2028 for some seats); depending on the local organization plan, members represent the whole school corporation (at large) or a district within it, and some boards are wholly or partly appointed [2]
- **Ballot label:** Each candidate chooses a party label, 'Independent', or no affiliation, shown next to the name; no primary and no straight-ticket voting for these races [2, 4]
- **Pay:** Set by board resolution: an annual amount of no more than 10% of the lowest starting teacher salary in the school corporation, plus a capped per diem [5]
- **On the 2026 ballot:** 632 races in 299 school corporations; 1,039 candidates [6]

### What the office does

- Hires, evaluates and can dismiss the superintendent, and approves the hiring and firing of principals, teachers and other staff recommended by the superintendent [1, 7, 3].
- Adopts the annual budget, appropriates funds and oversees school finances [1, 3].
- Decides whether to ask voters to approve an operating referendum (an extra property tax levy); the board adopts the resolution and the Department of Local Government Finance reviews the ballot language for compliance with state law [8].
- Approves contracts, buys and sells property, and decides on construction, repair and demolition of school buildings [1].
- Bargains with the teachers' union over salary, wages and wage-related benefits such as health insurance and retirement; state law excludes teacher evaluation procedures from those bargaining subjects [9].
- Writes and updates policies and rules for schools, often in a policy handbook, including the student handbook [1, 3].
- Buys curricular materials, provides them to students without charge, and runs programs such as student transportation and school lunch [1].
- Must adopt a process for parents and residents to ask that library material be removed as obscene or harmful to minors, review requests at public meetings and keep a public catalog of library materials [10].
- Hears written appeals of student expulsions, unless the board has voted not to hear them [11].
- Must take official action openly; Indiana's Open Door Law says public agencies' official action is to be conducted openly so the people may be fully informed [12, 13].
- A school board member must have lived in the school corporation (and, for a district seat, the district) for at least one year before the election, and a teacher or other employee of the school corporation may not serve on its board [2].
- What the board does not control: the State Board of Education adopts statewide academic standards [14], the General Assembly sets the state school funding formula in the state budget [15], and voucher (Choice Scholarship) eligibility is set by state law [16].

### Recent changes to the office

- 2025: Senate Enrolled Act 287 allowed school board candidates to declare a party affiliation; the Indiana School Boards Association opposed the change, and a supporter quoted by WNDU, a Noblesville school board member, said it adds transparency [17].
- 2026: Candidates choose a party, 'Independent' or no affiliation on their nominating petition, and that choice appears on the ballot; a candidate claiming the Democratic or Republican party must have voted in that party's primary in the two most recent primaries they voted in, or have certification from the county party chair, or can be challenged and removed from the ballot [2].
- July 2026: Chalkbeat reported, citing Indiana School Boards Association data, that 524 of 1,044 candidates chose a party, about 72% of them Republicans [18]; the Indiana Election Division's 2026 guidance and the wording of the law differ on the primary-voting test, a question county election boards decide [19].
- February 2026: Senate Enrolled Act 25 set the order of school board candidates on the ballot, grouped by party, with candidates who list no affiliation placed after the party and independent candidates [20].
- 2025: Senate Enrolled Act 1, a property tax law, limits how much operating revenue districts can raise through taxes and restricts school referendums to statewide general elections in even-numbered years [21, 22].
- 2025: The same law requires districts to share operating revenue with brick-and-mortar charter schools that enroll their students, statewide starting in 2028 [21, 22].
- 2025: The state budget opened Choice Scholarship vouchers to all Indiana families starting in June 2026 [16].
- 2026: The Indianapolis Public Education Corporation, rather than the Indianapolis Public Schools board, now certifies Indianapolis Public Schools referendum questions for the ballot [20].
- 2023: Senate Enrolled Act 391 changed the so-called $1 law so the Indiana Department of Education can order districts that lost at least 10% of enrollment over five years to close schools under 60% capacity and offer them to charter schools [23].
- 2023: House Enrolled Act 1447 required school districts to set up procedures for complaints that library material is obscene or harmful to minors [10].

## This race

- The district: South Vermillion Community School Corporation, based in Clinton, operates South Vermillion High School, South Vermillion Middle School, Central Elementary and Van Duyn Elementary [24]. Superintendent Dr. Micah Williams, a former SVMS teacher and the district's assistant superintendent since August 2023, was promoted to superintendent in July 2025 [25]; he succeeded Dave Chapman, who had led the district since 2008 [26]. In August 2026 Williams reported that enrollment came in below the district's projection of about 1,550 students and was lower than the prior year [27].
- The board has seven members [24, 28]. Seats are tied to residence areas, Clinton City, Clinton Township, Helt Township and one at-large seat, with Clinton City, Clinton Township and Helt Township seats elected in 2024 and the at-large seat plus one seat from each of the three areas elected in 2022 [29, 30]. The 2026 sample ballot for a Clinton City precinct includes all four South Vermillion races, each 'Vote For One (1) Only' [31]. Current members are John Paul Roehm (president), Kelli Hill (vice president), Jon Beardsley (secretary), Jeff Goeppner, Jeff Sisson, Brandae Stateler and Bruce West (legislative liaison) [24, 32].
- Seats on the 2026 ballot: four seats, one each for At Large, Clinton City, Clinton Township and Helt Township, matching the state list [6, 31]. Three incumbents are running again: Jeff Sisson (At Large, elected 2022 over Christina M. Barnes), Brandae Stateler (Clinton Township, elected 2022 over Jon E. Beardsley, 1,532 to 1,500) and board president John Paul Roehm (Helt Township, unopposed in 2022) [29, 33].
- Clinton City seat: no candidate filed for the Clinton City seat in 2022 [29]. At the January 2023 reorganization, Roehm stated that Beardsley was 'a holdover member from his previous appointment on the board' and did not need to be sworn in [28]. The minutes do not name Beardsley's seat; that he holds this Clinton City seat is an inference. Beardsley is not on the 2026 ballot, so the Clinton City race between Bryan Alan Lemmons and Meghan L. Bell will seat a new member [6].
- The district's other Clinton City seat, won in 2024 by Jerry Hawkins, became vacant in 2025 [30, 34]. On Aug. 20, 2025, after interviewing five applicants, including 2026 candidate Bryan Lemmons, the board appointed Jeff Goeppner 3–2 over Nick Hall [34]. Williams said that appointment runs through the remainder of Hawkins' term, which expires in 2028 [34].
- School reconfiguration (2026): at public information meetings on June 10 and June 24, 2026, Williams presented options to renovate Central and Van Duyn elementaries or to reconfigure the district into a PK–6 building and a 7–12 building on one campus for the 2029–2030 school year; he said current school capacity use is 52.89% [35, 36]. The district's FAQ says either option would use the same bonding capacity in 2028 and that overall tax rates would not be affected. It also says no staff would lose their jobs because of the reconfiguration, and it lists plans to bring back elementary art and add grade 2 high-ability programming [37]. Residents at the June 10 meeting raised traffic concerns at Wildcat Drive and IN-63, and Williams said he would follow up with INDOT [35, 37]. The board had discussed school consolidation in executive session on Dec. 1, 2025 [38].
- Budget (2025–2026): the 2026 budget cut the Education Fund by about $500,000, held the Operations Fund level, and raised the Debt Service Fund by about $300,000 to speed up planned debt repayment [39]. The proposed 2027 budget, advertised in August 2026, includes an Education Fund of about $12.72 million, about $1.3 million of which is planned for transfer to the Operations Fund; an Operations Fund of about $5.35 million with an advertised levy of about $4.6 million; and a debt service levy of $3 million [27]. Assessed valuation was reported at about $671.1 million, up about 1.31%, and the public hearing was set for Sept. 1, 2026 [27]. The board approved a 2026–27 classified employee handbook that includes a 2% increase in the compensation model [35].
- Enrollment (August 2026): Williams said the enrollment drop is expected to reduce projected funding, partly offset by an increase in state per-student funding, and that no elementary class exceeded about 22 students [27].
- Academics (August 2026): the district reported 2026 IREAD pass rates of about 98.2% at Van Duyn Elementary and about 76.7% at Central Elementary, and outlined Orton-Gillingham training and intervention steps for Central [27].
- Policies (2026): in June 2026 the board adopted policy updates requiring students to keep personal devices stored during instructional time, in line with new state law [35]. In August 2026 the district moved to two athletic directors sharing oversight of grades 5–12 [27].
- Forums: no candidate forum, questionnaire or news coverage of the 2026 South Vermillion races was found. The only recorded statements from challengers are Bryan Lemmons' August 2025 appointment interview and his July 2026 public comment to the board [34, 40].

## Bryan Alan Lemmons — Clinton City

Challenger for the Clinton City seat, which has no incumbent on the ballot. He applied for the board's 2025 Clinton City vacancy but was not chosen [34, 6].

### Background

- At an August 2025 board interview he described himself as retired from the military and from the State of Indiana, with supervisory experience in the military and the Department of Correction and a background in mental health and adversity response [34].
- Parent of students in the district; said he is active in Little League and youth sports [34].

### Path to the ballot

- Listed on the state candidate list as Nonpartisan [6].
- Was one of five applicants interviewed on Aug. 18, 2025 for the vacant Clinton City seat. On Aug. 20 the board's nominations and votes went to Jeff Goeppner and Nick Hall, and Goeppner was appointed [34].

### Positions & plans

**Academics**

- In his 2025 interview said board members should ensure curriculum follows state and board of education requirements and should raise academic standards to challenge students [34].
- Supported hands-on learning for future educators, with students involved in teaching [34].

**Teachers & staff**

- In a July 16, 2026 public comment to the board, identified as a board candidate, he called for added procedures and protocols for hiring coaches, more transparency in coach selection, and evaluation of coaching effectiveness [40].

**Transparency & community input**

- In 2025 said that when residents bring concerns he would listen and write them down without making promises, and would separate valid from invalid points before bringing them to the board [34].
- Said he would accept majority board decisions he disagreed with [34].

### On the current officeholder

- At the July 2026 meeting he raised concerns about the district's coaching hiring practices, declining participation in athletic programs, and parents' concerns that athletes were discouraged or penalized for family vacations during seasons. He said his comments were meant to encourage positive changes [40]. The board adopted a new 5–12 athletic director structure the next month; the minutes do not tie that change to his comments [27].

### How they say they'd run the office

- In his 2025 interview said his first-year priority would be boosting student morale, including encouraging participation in school events and sports and connecting community involvement with school activities [34].

### What we couldn't verify

- No 2026 campaign website, questionnaire or news coverage found; positions come from his 2025 appointment interview and his 2026 public comment as summarized in board minutes.
- No stated positions on the elementary reconfiguration plan or the budget found.
- No campaign finance reports reviewed.
