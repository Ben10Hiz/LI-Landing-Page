# Indiana County Sheriff — 2026 race profile

_Researched 2026-10-11 · status: draft._ Pending editorial review.

## About the office

The sheriff is an elected county officer created by the Indiana Constitution who arrests offenders, keeps the peace, serves court papers, keeps order in county courts and runs the county jail [1, 2]. The Indiana Sheriffs' Association describes sheriffs as constitutional officers elected by the people, whose work includes operating jails, serving civil process, providing court security and coordinating emergency response [3]. Every county elects its sheriff in 2026 for a four-year term, and no one may serve as sheriff more than eight years in any twelve-year period [1].

- **Term:** 4 years; the sheriff serves until a successor is elected and qualified [4, 1]
- **Elected:** Countywide by the county's voters on the partisan general election ballot; all county sheriffs are elected in 2026 [1]
- **Term limit:** No more than eight years in any period of twelve years (Indiana Constitution, Article 6, Section 2) [1]
- **Qualifications:** Must have lived in the county for at least one year before the election, be registered to vote in the county by the filing deadline, and file a statement of economic interests; a sheriff who moves out of the county forfeits the office [1, 4]
- **Pay:** A county may pay the sheriff a salary from the county general fund of at least 50% to 100% of the state minimum salary of a full-time prosecuting attorney, depending on county population [5]
- **On the 2026 ballot:** 92 races; 26 contested [6]

### What the office does

- Arrests without a warrant people who commit an offense in the sheriff's view, brings them before a county court, and holds them while the arrest is investigated [2].
- Suppresses breaches of the peace and pursues and jails felons [2].
- Takes care of the county jail and the prisoners held there [2, 3].
- Serves and carries out legal papers (process) directed to the sheriff by courts and the county commissioners [2].
- Attends and keeps order in all courts of the county, which the Sheriffs' Association describes as providing court security [2, 3].
- Takes photographs, fingerprints and other identification data from people held for crimes, and DNA samples for felonies [2].
- Reports twice a year to the Indiana Department of Correction the average daily cost of holding a jail prisoner [2].
- Holds state prisoners, including people convicted of Level 6 felonies, in the county jail, for which the state reimburses counties at a daily rate set in the state budget [7].
- Coordinates emergency response and works with local police, prosecutors and community partners [3].
- In a county whose council creates a sheriff's merit board by ordinance, appoints three of its five members, while the county police officers elect the other two; no more than two sheriff appointees and one elected member may belong to the same party [8].
- Must report information to federal authorities when a person arrested for a felony or misdemeanor is believed to lack legal immigration status, and, since July 2026, notify the judge setting bail when holding a person subject to an immigration detainer [9, 10].
- May supervise and inspect businesses such as pawnbrokers and secondhand dealers [2].
- What the sheriff does not control: the county commissioners must establish and maintain the county jail building [11]; the county makes the appropriation for feeding prisoners and sets the sheriff's salary [5]; the prosecuting attorney decides which crimes to prosecute [12]; and judges decide bail [13].

### Recent changes to the office

- February 2025: The Indiana Department of Correction said funding to reimburse county jails for holding Level 6 felons and state prisoners, then $40 per day, was exhausted for state fiscal year 2025; Tippecanoe County Sheriff Robert Goldsmith, then president of the Indiana Sheriffs' Association, called it very disappointing, and Rep. Greg Steuerwald (R-Avon) said everything owed would be paid [14].
- 2025: The new state budget raised the daily rate for Level 6 offenders to $42, raised the jail reimbursement line to $45 million and then $41 million a year, and moved to direct reimbursement with costs submitted within 60 days; Sheriffs' Association executive director Stephen Luce called the rate better than nothing, citing an association study that put the average cost at about $74 per inmate per day [7].
- July 2025: The Department of Correction planned to begin paying about $12.7 million owed to county jails for housing state offenders [7].
- July 2025: House Enrolled Act 1393 took effect, requiring jails to alert federal immigration authorities, with the county sheriff passing the information to federal officials, after felony or misdemeanor arrests when there is probable cause the person lacks legal status [9].
- July 2026: Senate Bill 76 took effect, requiring sheriff's offices and police departments to notify the bail judge when they hold a person subject to an immigration detainer and barring local policies that restrict cooperation with federal officials; St. Joseph County Sheriff Bill Redman said his county already cooperates with ICE, while South Bend Mayor James Mueller said the city will protect the constitutional rights of everyone living there [10].
- January 2026: The Sheriffs' Association's 2026 president, Porter County Sheriff Jeffrey Balon, listed jail overcrowding, staffing shortages, the drug epidemic and mental illness among the issues sheriffs raise with legislators [3].
- November 2026: Public Question 1 asks voters whether courts may deny bail, for offenses other than murder or treason, when the state proves by clear and convincing evidence that no release conditions will reasonably protect others or the community [15]; Sen. Eric Koch (R-Bedford) said releasing some defendants before trial is too dangerous, while Zach Stock of the Indiana Public Defender Council raised concerns about jail overcrowding [13].

## Current officeholder: Kerry Forestal (Democratic)

Kerry Forestal, a Democrat who has led the Marion County Sheriff's Office since 2018 and is in his second term, is term-limited and not on the ballot; he is running for Indiana Senate District 31 [16, 17, 6].

- Background: a Marion County law enforcement officer since 1977, he was appointed U.S. Marshal for the Southern District of Indiana by President Barack Obama in 2010 [17].
- September 3, 2025: at a City-County Council committee hearing on his 2026 budget, residents and some Democratic councilors pressed him to stop holding U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) detainees in the jail under a federal agreement paying $75 per detainee per day; about 700 people were held for ICE from January to July 2025. Forestal said he was following the law, citing Gov. Mike Braun's executive order and a letter from Attorney General Todd Rokita threatening to sue non-cooperating sheriffs, and said "I don't believe we can" when asked whether he could refuse the detainees. Council Republicans praised his jail staffing increases [18].
- November 2025: the office asked the City-County Council for a $6.8 million transfer to cover raises for deputies under a new collective bargaining agreement with the Indiana Fraternal Order of Police Labor Council that runs through 2028, plus new equipment [19].
- January 2026: after the jail reached its council-funded cap of 2,400 people (physical capacity about 2,900), the office said it would no longer hold ICE detainees beyond 48 hours. Forestal attributed the crowding partly to about 175 state-sentenced people awaiting transfer to the Indiana Department of Correction, which reimburses $42 a day against a cost of $82, creating a shortfall of about $2.7 million a year. He said "I think it'd be reckless for me to say we're not going to cooperate" with federal agencies [20].
- September 2026: the office asked a court to order the Department of Correction to accept felony-sentenced people within the five days it says state law requires; it reported 258 such people waiting in the jail, nearly 10% of the population, and estimated the gap between its roughly $85 daily cost and the state's $42 reimbursement at about $4 million a year [21].
- Criticism: in January 2026 the Rev. Fatima Yakubu-Madus criticized Forestal for not mounting a legal defense on the ICE holds [20]; Republican nominee Tom Vaughn has said deputies raised concerns about staffing, safety, promotions and leadership direction and that confidence in the office needs to be restored [22, 23].

## This race

- The Marion County sheriff runs the county jail, serves criminal warrants, protects county government buildings and courts, and maintains the sex offender registry, with more than 800 employees and a budget of roughly $140 million [16, 23].
- This is an open seat: Sheriff Kerry Forestal is term-limited and is running for the Indiana Senate [17].
- WFYI identifies jail overcrowding and the office's relationship with ICE as the two central issues for the office heading into the election [23].
- May 5, 2026 Democratic primary: Kelvis T. Williams, the office's executive officer, defeated jail Capt. Gregory Patrick with about 55% of the vote to 45% [24].
- No Republican ran in the primary; in July 2026 the Marion County Republican chair placed Southport Police Chief Tom Vaughn on the ballot [23, 22]. IndyPolitics described the county as roughly 65–35 Democratic [22].
- No general-election debate or forum between Williams and Vaughn was found as of October 11, 2026.
- Key differences: Williams presents his candidacy as continuing the work of past sheriffs and lists more sex-offender checks, higher staff pay, expanded jail programming and youth outreach [16, 24]; Vaughn emphasizes longer detention-officer training, contraband scanners, juvenile gun crime and closer cooperation with IMPD and surrounding agencies [22].

## Kelvis T. Williams

Kelvis Williams, the Democratic nominee, is deputy chief and executive officer of the Marion County Sheriff's Office, heading its Homeland Security Division, with about four decades in law enforcement [16, 24].

### Background

- Deputy chief and executive officer at the sheriff's office under Sheriff Kerry Forestal; heads the Homeland Security Division, which handles security for county buildings and large events such as the Indianapolis 500 [16, 24].
- About four decades in law enforcement, including service as an Indiana state trooper and as a counterterrorism official with the Indiana State Police [16].
- Holds a bachelor's degree in criminal justice from Martin University and attended the FBI National Academy and Northwestern University's School of Police Staff & Command [16].

### Path to the ballot

- Won the May 5, 2026 Democratic primary over Gregory Patrick, about 55% to 45% [24].

### Positions & plans

**Jail & corrections**

- Would expand self-development classes in the jail, including talks from formerly incarcerated speakers, and add de-escalation training for jail staff [16].

**Staffing & pay**

- Supports higher pay for employees and more training for jail staff [16, 24].

**Patrol & response**

- Would increase checks on registered sex offenders, which Mirror Indy reported occur every other month [16].

**Immigration enforcement**

- In the primary, supported ending the jail's housing of ICE detainees beyond short-term holds required under a new state law, a position Mirror Indy reported both Democratic candidates shared [16].

**Transparency**

- Said a civilian review board is not necessary because the office is already "extremely transparent" with the media and public [16].

### On the current officeholder

- Presents his candidacy as continuing the work of past sheriffs, including Frank Anderson and Kerry Forestal, saying "Each sheriff has taken our agency to the next level" [16].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Work with faith leaders and community groups on youth crime and other youth problems [16, 24].
- Address homelessness in response to the new state law criminalizing encampments, a priority Mirror Indy reported both Democratic candidates shared [16].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- Marion County Prosecutor Ryan Mears supported Williams in the primary, according to Mirror Indy [25].
- No campaign finance totals were found.

### What we couldn't verify

- No campaign website or general-election platform was found; positions come from primary-season coverage.
- No general-election statements found on jail overcrowding, the Department of Correction transfer dispute, or how he would handle ICE holds in light of state requirements.
- No FOP or union endorsement found.

## Tom Vaughn

Tom Vaughn, the Republican nominee, has been Southport's police chief since 2014 and began his career in 1997 at the Marion County Sheriff's Department [22, 23].

### Background

- Southport police chief since 2014 [23, 22].
- Began in 1997 with the Marion County Sheriff's Department, working in the jail, court security and county facility security, and later joined the Indianapolis Park Rangers; IndyPolitics describes him as a 30-year law enforcement veteran [22].
- Describes himself as a lifelong Marion County resident [26].

### Path to the ballot

- No Republican filed in the May primary; the Marion County Republican Party chair appointed Vaughn to the general-election ballot, announced in July 2026 [23, 22].

### Positions & plans

**Jail & corrections**

- Lists improving "safety, training, and professionalism inside the Marion County Jail" as his top goal; says the three-week detention-staff training is insufficient and supports more training, equipment and technology, including full-body scanners to reduce contraband [22].

**Staffing & pay**

- Says deputies have raised concerns about staffing, personal safety, promotions and leadership direction [22].

**Patrol & response**

- Says the IMPD–Sheriff's Office merger has not worked as intended and the office has gradually given up core duties; calls for better cooperation among the sheriff's office, IMPD, excluded cities and surrounding communities, saying "crime is regional" [22].
- Cites rising juvenile crime; wants more consistent accountability for young offenders found with guns and suggests parents should sometimes be held responsible when minors easily get firearms at home [22, 23].

### On the current officeholder

- Has cited "increasing demands on our jail system" and the need to restore confidence in the Sheriff's Office [23, 26]; says deputies have raised concerns about leadership direction [22].

### How they say they'd run the office

- Lengthen and improve detention-staff training and add contraband-detection technology in the jail [22].
- Improve coordination with IMPD and neighboring departments [22].

### Endorsements & fundraising

- Announced his campaign through a press release issued by the Indy Republicans [23].
- No endorsements or campaign finance totals were found.

### What we couldn't verify

- No campaign website found.
- No stated position found on ICE detainer holds, the Department of Correction transfer backlog, or jail capacity limits.
- Fox59 announcement article carried no exact publication date (shown as about three months old in October 2026).
