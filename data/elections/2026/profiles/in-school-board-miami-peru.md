# Indiana School Board Member — 2026 race profile

_Researched 2026-10-10 · status: draft._ Pending editorial review.

## About the office

A school board is the elected or appointed governing body of a public school corporation, the legal unit that runs a local school district [1, 2]. The board hires and evaluates the superintendent, adopts policies, and approves the annual budget, while the superintendent and staff run schools day to day [3]. Most boards have five or seven members serving staggered four-year terms [3]. Starting in 2026, candidates may list a political party, run as independents, or list no affiliation on the ballot, and there is no primary for these seats [2, 4].

- **Term:** 4 years, with elections staggered so part of the board is chosen every two years [3]
- **Elected:** At the November general election (November 3, 2026, or November 2028 for some seats); depending on the local organization plan, members represent the whole school corporation (at large) or a district within it, and some boards are wholly or partly appointed [2]
- **Ballot label:** Each candidate chooses a party label, 'Independent', or no affiliation, shown next to the name; no primary and no straight-ticket voting for these races [2, 4]
- **Pay:** Set by board resolution: an annual amount of no more than 10% of the lowest starting teacher salary in the school corporation, plus a capped per diem [5]
- **On the 2026 ballot:** 632 races in 299 school corporations; 1,039 candidates [6]

### What the office does

- Hires, evaluates and can dismiss the superintendent, and approves the hiring and firing of principals, teachers and other staff recommended by the superintendent [1, 7, 3].
- Adopts the annual budget, appropriates funds and oversees school finances [1, 3].
- Decides whether to ask voters to approve an operating referendum (an extra property tax levy); the board adopts the resolution and the Department of Local Government Finance reviews the ballot language for compliance with state law [8].
- Approves contracts, buys and sells property, and decides on construction, repair and demolition of school buildings [1].
- Bargains with the teachers' union over salary, wages and wage-related benefits such as health insurance and retirement; state law excludes teacher evaluation procedures from those bargaining subjects [9].
- Writes and updates policies and rules for schools, often in a policy handbook, including the student handbook [1, 3].
- Buys curricular materials, provides them to students without charge, and runs programs such as student transportation and school lunch [1].
- Must adopt a process for parents and residents to ask that library material be removed as obscene or harmful to minors, review requests at public meetings and keep a public catalog of library materials [10].
- Hears written appeals of student expulsions, unless the board has voted not to hear them [11].
- Must take official action openly; Indiana's Open Door Law says public agencies' official action is to be conducted openly so the people may be fully informed [12, 13].
- A school board member must have lived in the school corporation (and, for a district seat, the district) for at least one year before the election, and a teacher or other employee of the school corporation may not serve on its board [2].
- What the board does not control: the State Board of Education adopts statewide academic standards [14], the General Assembly sets the state school funding formula in the state budget [15], and voucher (Choice Scholarship) eligibility is set by state law [16].

### Recent changes to the office

- 2025: Senate Enrolled Act 287 allowed school board candidates to declare a party affiliation; the Indiana School Boards Association opposed the change, and a supporter quoted by WNDU, a Noblesville school board member, said it adds transparency [17].
- 2026: Candidates choose a party, 'Independent' or no affiliation on their nominating petition, and that choice appears on the ballot; a candidate claiming the Democratic or Republican party must have voted in that party's primary in the two most recent primaries they voted in, or have certification from the county party chair, or can be challenged and removed from the ballot [2].
- July 2026: Chalkbeat reported, citing Indiana School Boards Association data, that 524 of 1,044 candidates chose a party, about 72% of them Republicans [18]; the Indiana Election Division's 2026 guidance and the wording of the law differ on the primary-voting test, a question county election boards decide [19].
- February 2026: Senate Enrolled Act 25 set the order of school board candidates on the ballot, grouped by party, with candidates who list no affiliation placed after the party and independent candidates [20].
- 2025: Senate Enrolled Act 1, a property tax law, limits how much operating revenue districts can raise through taxes and restricts school referendums to statewide general elections in even-numbered years [21, 22].
- 2025: The same law requires districts to share operating revenue with brick-and-mortar charter schools that enroll their students, statewide starting in 2028 [21, 22].
- 2025: The state budget opened Choice Scholarship vouchers to all Indiana families starting in June 2026 [16].
- 2026: The Indianapolis Public Education Corporation, rather than the Indianapolis Public Schools board, now certifies Indianapolis Public Schools referendum questions for the ballot [20].
- 2023: Senate Enrolled Act 391 changed the so-called $1 law so the Indiana Department of Education can order districts that lost at least 10% of enrollment over five years to close schools under 60% capacity and offer them to charter schools [23].
- 2023: House Enrolled Act 1447 required school districts to set up procedures for complaints that library material is obscene or harmful to minors [10].

## This race

- The 'Miami Peru School Board' race is for the board of Peru Community Schools (Peru Community School Corporation), the public school district for Peru in Miami County [24, 25]. It runs Elmwood (K–2) and Blair Pointe Upper Elementary, Peru Junior-Senior High School and Keys Academy, an alternative school [25, 26]. Wikipedia lists enrollment at about 2,323 students; the Kokomo Tribune reported in December 2025 that Peru had the steepest enrollment drop among its area districts, down 100 students since fall 2024 [25, 27]. Trent McCormick has been superintendent since August 2024, after the resignation of Superintendent Jaime Cole [24, 28].
- Board: the board has seven members [29, 30]. Seats elected in 2022 (three) and 2024 (four, including the Erie Township seat) make up the board; after the 2024 election, Meg Guthrie-Pennington, Matthew Fuller and Jarrod Ellis joined, and Gregory Quin kept the Erie Township seat because no one filed for it [29, 31, 32]. In September 2026 the Peru Tribune identified Guthrie-Pennington as board vice president [33].
- Seats on the 2026 ballot: the state list shows one race, 'Peru 2-3-5-6-7-9 and Washington 1,' with five candidates and 'vote for 1' [6]. In 2022, three seats were filled together: the Kokomo Tribune reported 'three spots' on the board, held by Jarrod Ellis, Brittany Justice and Charles Wagner, with challengers Tommie Beattie and David Hoover, and reported results by share of the vote, with Hoover and Beattie first and second and Justice third [34, 30]. Those three four-year terms end this year, and Justice, Hoover and Beattie are all on the 2026 ballot [34, 6]. This suggests voters will again fill three seats; the actual number to be elected was not confirmed in an official 2026 source.
- Who holds the seats now: Brittany Justice (who said in 2022 she had served four years on the board, and was reelected that year), David Hoover and Tommie Beattie (both first elected 2022) [34, 30]. All three are running, listed as Republican; challengers are Brian A. Arwood (nonpartisan) and George Morris (Republican) [6].
- Party labels: the state candidate list shows Justice, Hoover, Beattie and Morris with the Republican label and Arwood as nonpartisan [6].
- Facilities and the $23.5 million plan (July 2025): the board voted 5–2 to buy the Roxy Theater on the north side of Peru and convert it into administration, transportation and maintenance offices at its Tiger North Campus, as part of a $23.5 million district-wide improvement project; Justice and Quin voted no [26, 35]. Buying and renovating the Roxy was estimated at about $5.7 million; the plan also includes demolishing the two-story classroom wing of the old junior high (about $1.5 million) and selling the Third Street administration building [26]. The district said it would finance the work with bonds replacing debt being paid off, keeping a stable tax rate; some community members, including Roxy employees, opposed the purchase [35].
- Ivy Tech lease (February 2026): the board voted unanimously to lease space in the former junior high building to Ivy Tech Community College, with the move expected by fall 2026; McCormick said it would help students get a head start on job skills and college credits [36].
- Budget pressure and Heartland Career Center (September 2026): on Sept. 21, 2026 the board voted to let McCormick explore leaving the Heartland Career Center cooperative and build its own career and technical programs, a process that could run through June 2028 [33]. The Peru Tribune reported the move was driven by a $2.5 million budget deficit and declining enrollment, and framed by school leaders as a way to avoid a tax referendum [33]. McCormick said it was 'primarily... a financial move' because the district pays for Heartland slots it is not filling; Heartland's director asked the board to reconsider, and board member Fuller said he hoped for 'some kind of equitable agreement' [33].
- Property-tax law: the Kokomo Tribune reported that Peru expected $15–16 million in bonds in 2026 with the rest likely in 2028 because of the new state property-tax law, and that lower tax rates and other limits on property-tax revenue have raised concerns for schools about facility upkeep and operating costs [26].
- Enrollment and school choice: McCormick told the Kokomo Tribune that Indiana's school choice options affect traditional schools and that many students are choosing online schools [27].
- Forums: no 2026 candidate forum or newspaper questionnaire for this race was found. The most recent questionnaire answers from three of the candidates (Justice, Hoover and Beattie) are from the Kokomo Tribune's 2022 guide and are labeled as such below [34].

## Brittany Justice — Peru 2-3-5-6-7-9 and Washington 1

Incumbent, reelected in 2022 after a first term; listed as Republican [34, 30, 6].

### Background

- In 2022 she said she has worked with children since age 19 and moved from private childcare to public education [34].
- She cited experience as deputy trustee for the Washington Township trustee, seasonal work at the Ivy Tech Kokomo bookstore, and handling the accounting for a local appliance repair business [34].

### Path to the ballot

- Appears on the state candidate list with a Republican label [6].
- Reelected in 2022, finishing third for the three seats [30].

### Positions & plans

**Facilities**

- Voted against buying the Roxy Theater in July 2025, calling it 'a want' rather than a need and arguing the old junior high building, already owned, could be renovated for the same purpose [26].
- Voted against the other resolutions for the $23.5 million project, saying the $5.735 million for the Roxy 'could have gone into the other building project — or not spent at all to give my constituents a break' [35].
- In 2022 she said bond money should be divided fairly among areas needing renovation, naming athletics, food service and traffic at Elmwood [34].

**School safety**

- In 2022 she named student and staff safety as a top issue [34].

**Teachers & staff**

- In 2022 she said the district should budget to raise base pay and benefits to compete with nearby schools and build a supportive workplace to retain staff [34].

**Academics**

- In 2022 she said she is not a fan of online learning except when in-person school is not possible [34].

### On the current officeholder

- Is a current board member [34, 30]. In 2022 she said she had supported some board budgeting decisions and opposed others [34]. In July 2025 she was one of two votes against the Roxy purchase and the related project resolutions [35].
